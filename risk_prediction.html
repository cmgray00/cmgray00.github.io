Risk Prediction in Higher Education with Machine Learning

During my MS Data Science capstone, I'd designed and deployed this machine learning pipeline to predict D/F/Withdrawal risk in developmental college math. Outputs enabled targeted interventions and improved data-driven decision making in higher education.

Deliverables included:

  1. A 1-page targeted highlights and suggestions e-mail
  2. An 87-page (double-spaced, before references and appendices) paper for course and client
  3. A findings presentation with Q&A session
  4. A set of scripts automating course setup and certain maintenance tasks, with training session
  5. A set of scripts stepping through extraction, cleansing, and modeling data for ongoing risk predictions, with commented instructions and SOP
  6. The Excel output file containing predictions and justifications

I selectively share highlights from the findings while featuring interesting pre-processing steps not typically included on this portfolio.

In the end, logistic regression achieved 87.9% accuracy with an 89.5 F1-score using repeated cross-validation (100× 5-fold). It balanced outputs across demographics, reducing bias while retaining predictive power.

Introduction

Developmental math courses help bridge the gap between pre-college and college-level math skills. Across most U.S. universities, these courses have long struggled with high D/F/Withdrawal (DFW) rates, where students earn poor grades or withdraw after the drop deadline.

Despite evidence-based changes implemented by the client over several years, high DFW rates persisted. This has serious consequences: delaying (sometimes indefinitely) a student’s graduation and entrance into the workforce.

Project motivation: Understand what drives DFW risk at this institution and create an early risk prediction alert tool to support proactive interventions in future semesters.

About the Data

Course performance data was collected via Canvas LMS APIs, capturing time- and score-based records. The dataset spans Fall 2022 through Spring 2024 to ensure consistency and relevance.

Attendance data came from Excel downloads of clock punch timestamps. Ideally, students clocked in and out once per date — but frequent errors required thoughtful data cleansing and decision-making to define presence accurately.

Demographic data was drawn from two sources:

  • The Registrar’s records (obtained under strict FERPA compliance controls)
  • Course catalog + faculty feedback, used to classify academic plans (e.g., STEM, relative math demands)

Client conversations and a targeted literature review provided critical context, helping define the data approach and ensure its responsible interpretation.

Process and Findings, in a nutshell

Extensive preprocessing and feature engineering were guided by client expertise and literature findings. These steps homogenized data meaning across terms and extracted valuable signals about student behaviors - like assessment preparedness and homework patterns in the short- and long-term.

The model focused on predicting DFW risk rather than rate. This distinction addressed a core challenge: students may improve mid-course thanks to untracked support efforts (i.e. tutoring or study groups.) Traditional accuracy metrics for rate prediction would unfairly penalize the model in such cases. By defining “sticky risk” (once in risk, always in risk), we avoid this issue and ensure good interpretability.

Building temporal granularity was key. End-of-term grade snapshots were converted into weekly records using custom grading policies. Such policies were stricter than those in the live class to unmask risk. For example, the lowest few assessment scores were no longer dropped. Still, they were fair: students weren't penalized for normal grading delays.

Live Grading Policies Provide a Significant Weekly Score Boost, Masking Struggling Performance

We wished to avoid over-flagging students who would recover under the live, more forgiving grading policies. Using letter grade segmentation, a more precise score difference for D-students informed a new risk threshold setting.

To label DFW risk status with integrity, timing was important. For example, students may join the class late, or lack a sufficient collection of graded assignments during the initial weeks. Poor quality records weren't used.

Exploratory analysis and associative rules identified risk signals and suggested preferences among similar features. At-risk students often fell behind on homework, especially as the course progressed:

Feature relationships shifted over time, making weekly models more effective than a single generalized one. Week 6 emerged as the optimal timeframe to perform predictions, as it balanced many requirements. Accurate predictions were to be made as early as possible, while minimizing instances of missed at-risk students:

Highlights:

  • The "checkmark" pattern formed by "risk history" background bars and black lined "naïve prevalence" occurs because the number of students described by good quality data was starkly changing until at least week four.
    • Naïve prevalence was a “no information” baseline – the accuracy resulting from flagging all students for DFW risk.
    • Risk history was another baseline – the guaranteed accuracy provided by that sticky risk status alone.
  • This model accuracy significantly outperformed both the naïve prevalence and sticky risk baselines.
  • Logistic regression at Week 6 achieved 87.9% accuracy and 89.5 F1-score (via 100× 5-fold cross-validation), while balancing outputs across demographics.
  • Nine DFW risk drivers were compared and discussed.

Misclassification patterns revealed:

  • Well-performing students occasionally struggled on the cumulative final, raising hypotheses about independent review gaps or test anxiety.
  • Students near the threshold were common - a natural challenge in classification.
  • Unpredictable personal circumstances contributed to some withdrawals.

Incorporating demographic features reduced over-flagging of certain minority groups without hurting accuracy - producing unbiased classifications by segmentation. Stark behavioral differences were seen across some demographic segments, promoting two new perspectives from which to view course components and broader "introduction to college life" initiatives at the university.

The output is an Excel file listing students by intervention priority, contextual evidence, and proximity to the risk threshold (a custom metric) - supporting confident decisions. Class leadership is empowered to see and cluster common trends. Post-course, comparisons of risk vs. rate provided valuable improvement tracking for future terms.

Reflections

This project reinforced how critical collaboration with subject matter experts is in building a meaningful, ethical model. Conversations with the client and her colleagues - along with insights from the literature - shaped key decisions on data selection, feature engineering, and risk labeling. These inputs ensured the model reflected real-world dynamics rather than assumptions.

Working within FERPA constraints was both a challenge and an opportunity. It demanded thoughtful choices about what data to request, how to anonymize it, and how to balance richer analysis with privacy protections. The complexity of obtaining and managing sensitive data initially added effort, but ultimately strengthened the integrity of the solution. Future phases could streamline this process with leaner data inputs while retaining predictive power.

Limitations and considerations:

  • The dataset came from a predominantly Caucasian university, with developmental math students mainly pursuing degrees with low- to average- math requirements. Results may not generalize well to institutions with different demographics or program structures.
  • Canvas API data changed over time for students who eventually withdrew, limiting model learning from this important group in the earlier semesters.
  • The course underwent structural changes after the analyzed semesters. The recommended model should be revalidated before applying it to newer data.

Above all, this work deepened my appreciation for the complexity of student success. Macro trends pointed to clear risk factors, but micro trends reminded me of the human stories behind the data. I’m proud to have contributed responsibly to a tool aimed at supporting students’ growth and success.
