Risk Prediction in Higher Education with Machine Learning

The finale of my MS Data Science degree was entitled "Identifying Key Student Attributes Associated with DFW Status Risk in a Developmental College Math Course." I delivered findings and tools to the client in a few ways:

  1. A 1-page targeted highlights and suggestions e-mail
  2. An 87-page (double-spaced, before references and appendices) paper for course and client
  3. A findings presentation with Q&A session
  4. A set of scripts automating course setup and certain maintenance tasks, with training session
  5. A set of scripts stepping through extraction, cleansing, and modeling data for ongoing risk predictions, with commented instructions and SOP
  6. The automated Excel output file containing predictions and contextual classification justifications.

I'm selective about sharing the findings, but supplement some interesting pre-processing steps not normally featured on this portfolio.

Introduction

Developmental math courses in college boost a student’s math skills from pre-college to college level. Such courses have been suffering from high rates of students getting D’s, F’s, or Withdrawing beyond a course drop deadline (known as the DFW rate) across most US universities.

The client has already implemented the most promising solutions per literature-based studies over the years, yet the high rates persist. This has implications for delaying – sometimes indefinitely – a student from graduating and becoming part of the work force in the way they desire.

The motivation for this paper was to understand what drives DFW risk here, and to create an early risk prediction alert tool to support proactive student interventions in future semesters.

About the Data

Canvas LMS APIs were leveraged to pull time- and score-based course performance data. To ensure findings would be current and consistent, data from Fall 2022 to Spring 2024 inclusive was used.

Attendance data usually included datetime clock punches downloadable as an Excel file. Ideally, a student punched in and out once per date - but mistakes were frequent and required a combination of cleansing and decision-making to aptly define a student's presence.

Demographic data was derived from two sources. First, the university’s Registrar team provided some records after extensive controls for FERPA compliance. Second, the course catalogue website and professor feedback was used to create a new dataset classifying academic plans (i.e. a BA in Geospatial Analysis and Technology) in different ways (STEM field, relative math requirements, etc.)

Client conversations and learnings from a literature review informed extensive data pre-processing steps and feature engineering. These steps homogenized data meaning over time, and extracted more data value by quantifying student studying behaviors. Examples included short- and long-term perspectives on weekly assessment preparedness and homework cramming.

Process and Findings, in a nutshell

Modeling DFW risk instead of rate resolved a fundamental issue about what "good" results were amid a class that constantly offers untraced support events. The traditional accuracy calculation for DFW rate would suffer in the presence of students who improved as the course progressed. We want students to improve - but that also counterintuitively implies we “want” as many predicted DFW's to go away; lower prediction accuracy. Disentangling improvements from predictions was possible using “sticky risk” instead of rate. "Sticky" because, once a student slipped into risk territory, they retained that status until the end - regardless of whether they improved signficantly thereafter. Now, our inability to trace support events is irrelevant, and risk accuracy isn't penalized when a student improves.

Tracking sticky risk required temporal data, so extensive pre-processing converted the end-of-course snapshot into more of a "live," weekly granularity using new "data processing only" grading policies. These were harsher than the real course grading policies (which could drop low grades in certain assignment categories) so as not to "mask" risky behaviors and performance. Still, student scores weren't punished for reasonable delays. For example, a student's assessment could be graded a few days after most others. The difference between scores derived from each set of policies is seen below:

Live Grading Policies Provide a Significant Weekly Score Boost, Masking Struggling Performance

The highest score masking by real course grading policies occured during the same timeframe we hoped to run predictions (somewhere between weeks 3 and 9). Thus, raw scores from custom grading policies should be more accurate for predicting risk.

Score differences were also assessed by letter grade segmentation. For example, dropping the lowest 3 assessment scores would typically benefit a D student more than it would a B student. The average boost for raw D-students informed a raw score risk threshold choice. Its value was less prone to flag students who would likely escape risk under the live course grading forgiveness policies.

The final decision concerning true DFW risk status labeling was determining when data had enough integrity to aptly describe each student. Students may join the class a few weeks late. In some semesters, the graders wanted to have all assessments in-hand before grading them. Any withdrawn students automatically received gradebook 0's instead of "earning" them. A custom calculation identified which weeks could be assessed for DFW risk status per student.

Exploratory data analysis and associative rules revealed many potential features to use or navigate during modeling. Most notably, at-risk students were far more likely to be unprepared for assessments by not completing all associated homework beforehand. This behavior worsened as the course progressed:

Let's break down a few highlights:

  • The "checkmark" pattern formed by "risk history" background bars and black lined "naïve prevalence" occurs because the number of students described by good quality data was starkly changing until (at least) week four.
  • Naïve prevalence was a “no information” baseline – the accuracy resulting from flagging all students for DFW risk. Our model accuracy hopes to be well above this.
  • Risk history was another baseline – the guaranteed accuracy provided by that sticky risk status alone. Again, we want our model accuracy to be well above this.
  • Across candidate weeks 3-9, predictive accuracy did vastly exceed both baselines, indicating how significantly the other chosen predictors contributed to an accurate classification outcome.

It was recommended to use Week 6's logistic regression model to predict DFW risk, as it balanced the business requirements of maximizing accuracy ASAP in the course, while minimizing under-flagged students (false negatives, or "missed" risk students). Nine DFW risk drivers were compared and discussed.

Model misclassification analysis reminded us that 100% accuracy isn't possible, especially when making predictions too early in the course:

  • Week 6 occurs before the major holidays (Thanksgiving or Spring Break) - typical timeframes of some performance fatigue.
  • Commonly, well-performing students suffered on the cummulative final exam alone - sprouting theories around poor or neglected independent review of early-course concepts, or large test anxiety (seen in the literature).
  • Also commonly, many students hovered around the risk threshold.
  • Unexpected and unrelated-to-course-performance events are also known to cause student withdrawls.

Including demographic data yielded a few more interesting trends that either agreed or conflicted with literature. In EDA, specific minority groups had a significantly more difficult time keeping up with homework than others, prompting questions around the online homework platform's accessibility and potential cultural differences. In misclassification analysis, certain minority groups were over-flagged for risk. Incorporating their demographic identitifiers into a modified model would reduce excessive over-flagging while keeping overall accuracy virtually the same. Such a model achieved 87.9% accuracy and an 89.5 F1-score, indicating a very well-performing risk predictor tool. Balanced outputs per demographic segmentation indicated unbiased classifications, too. For the data scientists out there: performance metrics were derived via 100 iterations of repeated 5-fold cross-validation.

The output tool:

Upon choosing a week to run the model, the script automatically generated the favored Excel file format of risk predictions alongside contextual explanation. Students were ordered by prioritized intervention, and could be filtered on similar evidence (i.e. "this group isn't doing their homework and their assessment scores are suffering.") Guiding a sense of "confidence" in the risk prediction, a custom metric informed the reviewer how close the prediction was to the decision threshold. If run after course-end, the output included additional columns to compare risk against rate. For the first time, improvement rates could be quantified and monitored. Other university initiatives could be easily meshed with this output to hasten response times.

Some limitations exist:

  • These findings were derived from a university that's predominantly Caucasian, whose developmental math course population pursues academic plans with low- to average- math requirement levels. Findings may not translate well to universities with significantly different demographic distributions or class structures.
  • Canvas API record scope changed over time for students who eventually withdrew. About half of the withdrawn students from earlier semesters were not represented well for models to learn from.
  • The latest semester (beyond this report's data range) has a new structural change which may be incompatible with this recommended model. Prediction accuracy should be assessed on the new data before confidently applying its outputs.

Reflection

Complying with FERPA considers data type, personnel access levels, and usage intentions. This had an effect on how much effort and collaboration it took to obtain data, how it was used, and what could (and couldn't) end up in the final model or its outputs.

On the one hand, sensitive student data is protected from careless and potentially damaging outcomes. On the other, I see how this complexity can silo departments and deter rich analyses. The client and I decided to retain extra anonymization and complexity in exchange for a richer analysis, knowing that the tool's user experience would somewhat suffer in this first development phase. Results showed the process could be simplified in a second phase, as we'd need considerably less data.

I loved working with demographic data. Psychology, culture, diversity - humans are fascinatingly complex. While much risk could be described through macrotrends, microtrends require different solutions. What a privilege to be trusted with this data, and use it to responsibly support the budding talent of the next generation!
